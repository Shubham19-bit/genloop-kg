2. How I built it

Data. I picked 35 large US companies, about nine each from tech, financials, consumer and healthcare, so that questions like "which bank has the most assets" make sense. For each one I took the latest 10-K whose fiscal year ended between May 2025 and February 2026, the closest I could get to "the same year" across different fiscal calendars. Every fact stores its exact year-end date. The SEC publishes 10-Ks as web pages, so I printed each one to PDF with a headless browser. Wells Fargo's statements sit in a separate exhibit, which I merged into its PDF. Everything after that reads only the PDFs.

The graph. The key decision was to make every fact its own node instead of a simple link like "Apple, revenue, 416,161". A fact node stores the value, unit, scale, year and the exact table row it came from, and it links to the page of the filing where I found it. That keeps every number tied to its source page. A dictionary of about 60 wordings ("Total net sales", "Total revenue, net of interest expense", "Sales to customers" and so on) maps onto 12 standard metrics, so revenue can be compared across companies that label it differently. Auditors, cities and states, sectors, CEOs and segments are shared nodes, and they are what join the 35 filings into one graph for multi-hop questions. Ratios like margins are calculated when asked and cite both inputs. The graph is stored in NetworkX and has 1,126 nodes and 2,784 edges.

Getting facts out of the PDFs. I did not let an LLM read numbers. The 382 numeric facts (340 read from tables, 30 employee counts and 12 computed liabilities) come from code that rebuilds table rows from word positions on the page, finds the three main financial statements by their titles, picks the current-year column from the header, applies "in millions", and matches each row label against the dictionary, preferring totals over sub-lines. Where a company prints no "total liabilities" line, I compute it as assets minus equity and mark it as computed. Auditor (34/35 found) and employee count (30/35) also come from pattern matching. For the text facts (headquarters, CEO, state of incorporation, segments) I used a small local model, Qwen 3.5 9B through Ollama, and showed it only two or three likely pages. It has to return an exact quote and page number, and the code throws the fact away if that quote is not actually on the page.

Answering a question. The LLM never writes the answer. The same local Qwen model only turns the question into a short JSON plan, using the graph's own list of companies and metrics, built from eight steps (pick companies, get a metric, compute a ratio, rank, filter, sum, take a difference, follow a relation). Plain Python then runs that plan on the graph, with no vector or text-chunk search anywhere, and returns the answer, the facts it touched and their pages. If a fact is missing, the system says it has no answer instead of guessing.

The benchmark. I wrote 30 questions, ten of each type, before running the system on any of them, and made sure they touch every metric, relation and sector. An AI assistant helped me draft the wording, which I then edited. To find the gold answers I used a different model from the one in my pipeline (Gemini, in chat) with a fixed set of written rules, one filing at a time. I then checked every gold number against the filing's XBRL tag (334 of 335 agree; the one difference is the same Bank of America cash definition) and read each text fact on its page myself. Winners, totals and ratios were computed in code from those checked values. For every question I stored the gold answer, every fact needed to justify it, and the source filing and page of each. For "highest X in a sector", that means X for every company in the sector, not just the winner. While building the gold answers, before the first run, I replaced two questions whose lines are not printed in the filings (Amazon's total liabilities, which my system computes but I could not check against a printed value, and Morgan Stanley's long-term debt), so all 30 have a checkable answer. Unanswerable questions are a gap I list below. Scoring allows 1% on numbers and 0.05 percentage points on ratios, matches companies by any of their names, and checks text answers on the key name.

What I changed after the first run. Four general rules, not question-specific patches. (1) The executor now accepts two plan shapes the model kept writing: a ratio of two earlier steps, and a difference between two steps. (2) One bad plan now fails only its own question instead of stopping the whole run. (3) The auditor search now looks a few pages past the heading, because long audit reports put the signature at the end. That took auditor coverage from 23 to 34 companies. (4) State abbreviations ("Purchase, NY") are expanded so state filters match.

3. Where it failed, and why

  • Q18, a confident wrong total. This is the failure I care about most. The question was the combined revenue of companies headquartered in New York state. The model never extracted Pfizer's headquarters, so only 7 of the 8 companies matched, and the system answered $595.5B instead of $658.1B with no warning. In a bank, a number that is quietly missing a piece is worse than no number.
  • Q10, wording the pattern did not know. Goldman reports a "headcount of 47,400", but my pattern looks for "employees". The system rightly said it had no answer, but the fact is still missing. Five of the 35 employee counts are missing for similar reasons.
  • Q24, the planner made up syntax. Asked which healthcare company has the highest net margin, it pointed to an earlier step with {"ref": "s2"}, a form the plan language does not have. The executor rejected it, so the question failed without a wrong answer.
  • Q19, right answer, partly my wording. My question said "the bank with the highest net income", but my gold facts counted all nine financials companies, including Visa and Mastercard, which are not banks. The planner picked six real banks and also added a step it did not need, giving 89% precision and 73% recall. The answer was correct because JPMorgan wins either way.
  • Extraction errors outside the benchmark. The quote check caught one invented fact: the model listed "Commercial Banking" as a JPMorgan segment, which is not on the page. It missed another: it listed Morgan Stanley's CFO as a second CEO, because her name really is on that signature page. Pfizer's and Target's headquarters are missing. Separately, Bank of America's auditor signature is an image, so my pattern matching could not read it.
  • Things that limit comparisons. Year-ends range from May 2025 to February 2026, a few metrics differ by sector (bank "cash", "borrowings" vs "long-term debt"), and banks and most pharma companies print no operating income. Every fact keeps its year-end date and original row label, so these differences show up in the answers.
  • Limits of my own benchmark. I wrote the questions knowing how the graph works, they lean towards numbers, and none are unanswerable, so 90% is probably higher than the system would score on unseen questions.

4. What I would do with more time

  • Warn when coverage is incomplete. Before summing or ranking, check that every candidate company actually has the fact being filtered on. If some do not, answer "partial: k of n companies". That alone would have flagged Q18.
  • Check plans before running them against a schema, and send any error back to the model for one retry (fixes Q24).
  • Get facts from two sources. Run both a regex rule and the model for each text fact and for employee counts, keep only the values they agree on, and add OCR for image-only signatures like Bank of America's. Use XBRL while building the graph to catch bad numbers early, still without using it to answer.
  • Store more context. Add several years and the 10-Qs, plus notes like debt maturities, and link each number to the MD&A paragraph that explains it, so "why did X change?" gets a cited answer.
  • Build a harder benchmark, written by someone who has not seen my design and including unanswerable questions, to test whether the system knows when to say no.
